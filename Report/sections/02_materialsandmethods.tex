\subsection{Study area}

%question regarding restriction of biotic dispersal?
The study area is located at two provinces in the south of Mexico, the province of Oaxaca and the province of Chiapas (fig. \ref{fig:studyarea}). 

The dry tropical forest site is located around Nizanda (16°39' N, 95°00' W), Oaxaca at an elevation of 90-120~m a.s.l. It has a mean annual temperature of 27.7°C and seasonal rainfall with a mean annual precipitation of 900~mm where 90\% of the total annual rainfall occurs between May and October. It is characterized as dry tropical deciduous forest \citep{hordijkLandUseHistory2024}.

The wet tropical forest is located around Loma Bonita (16°01' N, 90°55'), Chiapas, at an elevation of 115-300~m a.s.l. It has a mean annual temperature of 24°C and a mean annual precipitation of 3000~mm with a relative dry season between February and April accounting for less than 10\% of the total annual rainfall. The vegetation varies between semi-deciduous and lowland tropical rain forest \citep{hordijkLandUseHistory2024}.

\subsection{Seed data}
There are twenty (25~m x 25~m) research plots established in both the dry and wet tropical forest (fig. \ref{fig:studyarea}, \ref{fig:seedtraps}). These plots were installed on a recently abandoned (0-10 months following abandonment) pasture or agricultural land by the PANTROP project in March 2020 \citep{hordijkLandUseHistory2024}. In the neotropics, shifting agriculture, also known as slash and burn agriculture, has been a common agricultural practice which after depletion of resources results in land abandonment \citep{jakovacRoleLanduseHistory2021}. 

To measure the seed variables in both forest sites (wet and dry), four seed traps were established in each plot. In the dry forest, the seed traps were rectangular with the dimensions of 70~cm x 70~cm and in the wet tropical forest, the seed traps were circular with the diameter of 1~m. The seed traps were positioned 6~m from the plots’ edges in each corner of the plots (fig. \ref{fig:seedtraps}). The seeds were collected monthly for a duration of one year in 2023. The content of the four traps was combined into one sample per plot. All research plots were geo-referenced and identified with a research plot ID.

Subsequently, the collected seeds were separated into groups of unique species and identified per species. Based on literature, each species was described by their dispersal mode (biotic/abiotic) and guild (pioneer/shade-tolerant/generalist). The literature used for species description was based on the PANTROP project database collection composed from previous theses and scientific articles. The separation and identification of seed species was performed with a seed catalog provided by Universidad Nacional Autonóma de México (UNAM) and with the support of research assistant Marina Beatriz Hernandez Mendez, an expert in tropical wet forest seed identification, and Gerardo Luis Cervantes Jiménez, an expert in tropical dry forest seed identification. Not all species could be reliably identified and described due to ambiguity in appearance or lack of literature on certain species, resulting in missing values.

All research plots were georeferenced and buffered to account for the surrounding forest landscape, based on which the \ac{ROI} was defined. The extent of the study area is approximately 5~km x 5~km for the dry forest and 7~km x 6~km for the wet forest (fig. \ref{fig:studyarea}).


\subsubsection{Landsat data}%\\
% Why 30 years?
% is it a poblem that the research plots are included in the landscape??
%Remote sensing techniques have become an invaluable tool for assessment of landscape. With historical field data being scarce and interview-based data being unreliable and difficult to obtain, remote sensing techniques offer a robust method for analysis of landscapes with large spatial extents \citep{decuyperContinuousMonitoringForest2022}.
In this research, images from Landsat 5 \ac{TM}, Landsat 7 \ac{ETM+} and Landsat 8 \ac{OLI} were used to assess the forest landscape composition. The Landsat archive of the United States Geology Survey was selected due to its rich temporal coverage \citep{kennedyBringingEcologicalView2014, finerCombatingDeforestationSatellite2018}. 

The time series ranges from the beginning of 1992 to the end of 2022, consisting of 719 scenes (WRS-2 path/row: 20–21/49) for the dry tropical forest and 572 scenes (WRS-2 path/row: 22–23/48–49) for the wet tropical forest with a spatial resolution of 30~m x 30~m. The upper limit corresponds to the year before seed collection, as the aim was to assess the forest landscape conditions preceding seed dispersal.

Figure \ref{fig:satellitedata} visualizes the distribution of available Landsat satellite imagery data across the years of interest and date of a year for wet and dry forest. We can observe that in the early 2000s, there is a dip in the number of scenes available for both forest types. This is due to a failed mission of Landsat 6 \ac{ETM} and a scan line corrector failure of Landsat 7 \ac{ETM+} in 2003 which in combination with filtering out images with high cloud cover results in lower data availability \citep{gowardHistoricalRecordLandsat2006, scaramuzza2005landsat}. % the landsat 7 error

All atmospherically corrected Landsat Collection 2 Level-2 surface reflectance data were preprocessed and downloaded via the \ac{GEE} platform \citep{gorelickGoogleEarthEngine2017}. Preprocessing steps included selecting scenes with less than 70\% cloud cover, clipping to the \ac{ROI}, masking cloud, shadow, cirrus and snow using the quality assessment band, and calculating the surface reflectance index \ac{NDMI}. The \ac{NDMI} (eq. \ref{eq:NDMI}), derived from Landsat's near-infrared and short-wave infrared bands, was used due to its ability to detect gradual changes and regrowth more effectively than \ac{NDVI} \citep{vermotePreliminaryAnalysisPerformance2016}.

\begin{align}
\label{eq:NDMI}
NDMI = \frac{\text{NIR} - \text{SWIR}}{\text{NIR} + \text{SWIR}}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Land cover map}%\\
As the aim of this research was to evaluate the forest landscape surrounding the research plots, land cover map was used to mask out all non-forest areas. For this purpose we used the \ac{ESA} WorldCover map from 2021. The \ac{ESA} WorldCover 2021 is a global land cover map based on Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 data with tree cover land use class user's accuracy for North America of 80.6\% \citep{zanagaESAWorldCover102022, tsendbazar2021product}. The \ac{ESA} WorldCover map was preprocessed and downloaded via the \ac{GEE} platform \citep{gorelickGoogleEarthEngine2017, zanagaESAWorldCover102022}. The preprocessing steps included clipping the land cover map to the \ac{ROI} and rescaling the resolution to 30~m x 30~m to align with the satellite imagery using nearest neighbor interpolation to preserve categorical values. 








\subsection{AVOCADO algorithm}
The preprocessed landsat data were supplied to the \ac{AVOCADO} algorithm designed to detect forest disturbance and regrowth based on satellite imagery in a semi-automated and continuous manner \citep{decuyperContinuousMonitoringForest2022}. %\ac{AVOCADO} uses reference vegetation from a nearby pixel known to have remained undisturbed throughout the time series. 

The \ac{AVOCADO} algorithm consists of the following steps:

\textbf{1. Creating reference phenology} \\
The reference phenology is a phenological baseline representing an undisturbed forest of the same forest type and seasonality located outside of the \ac{ROI}. It was chosen with the help of Professor Jorge A. Maeve from the Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México, an expert in tropical forest ecology with a long term experience in the area. Using the \ac{NDMI} satellite imagery stack and the reference phenology extents, a stack of satellite imagery was extracted and compiled into a continuous vector consisting of \ac{NDMI} values for each pixel in the reference area. 
    %Similarly, a continuous temporal vector was created using the \ac{NDMI} acquisition dates to align each \ac{NDMI} value to its acquisition date. Subsequently, using the \textit{Phen()} function from the \texttt{npphen} R package, a reference phenology was created. 
The reference phenology was derived using kernel density estimation across both spatial and temporal dimensions. This involved aggregating all historical \ac{NDMI} annual cycles from every pixel within the reference area. The resulting distribution highlights the phenology distribution frequency of observed \ac{NDMI} values throughout the year.

\textbf{2. Time series anomaly and probability calculation}\\
Using the reference phenology, the \ac{NDMI} anomalies and anomaly likelihoods for each pixel in the \ac{ROI} was calculated. The \ac{NDMI} anomaly is understood as the difference between the observed \ac{NDMI} value from a given pixel and the \ac{NDMI} value from the reference phenology calculated for the respective date of the year. Moreover, the location of the observed \ac{NDMI} value within the reference distribution frequency is an indication for the anomaly likelihood. In other words, how likely it is that this anomaly is a true anomaly representing a forest disturbance \citep{decuyperContinuousMonitoringForest2022}. 

\textbf{3. Change detection and model adaptations}\\
The anomaly, anomaly likelihood expressed in percentiles and date were used in the final step of the \ac{AVOCADO} algorithm, the change detection. \ac{AVOCADO} algorithm detects two types of changes, disturbance and regrowth. Disturbance is defined as an \ac{NDMI} value falling outside of the \ac{RFD} threshold, whereas regrowth is defined as a recovery of an \ac{NDMI} value reaching again the reference phenology. %, more specifically, the mode of the frequency distribution or higher. 
Figure \ref{fig:avoexample} illustrates disturbance and regrowth detection of a single Landsat pixel. To calibrate the function in such a way that it accommodates this research and accounts for remote sensing data instability, the following arguments and their adjacent values were used: \ac{RFD} threshold, disturbance threshold, regrowth threshold and \ac{cdates}. The \ac{RFD} is an interval within which \ac{NDMI} value remains considered undisturbed vegetation. Given an \ac{RFD} threshold of 0.95, disturbance is considered to be the 5\% of values belonging outside of the 95\% interval. The higher \ac{RFD} threshold the more distinguishable the disturbance must be. Since in this study we were interested in absolute clearance of the forest (as oppose to drought disturbance), in order to assess the forest landscape, the \ac{RFD} threshold was set to 99\%. The \ac{cdates} argument defines the number of consecutive days the disturbance must be lasting for. This argument is added to account for noise in remote sensing data such as cloud cover. In this study we set \ac{cdates} to 3 following a proven example of \citet{decuyperContinuousMonitoringForest2022} and \citet{ ZHU201275}. If there are three consecutive dates with an \ac{NDMI} value falling outside of the \ac{RFD} range, the first occurrence is considered to be the beginning of the disturbance event. Lastly, disturbance and regrowth thresholds are arguments indicative of the number of calendar days that a disturbance or regrowth must be lasting for. In this research, the disturbance threshold is set to 730 days to avoid seasonal changes such as El Ni\~{n}o and the regrowth threshold is set to 365 to avoid detecting crop regrowth.

\subsection{Deriving forest age}
The output of the \ac{AVOCADO} algorithm was a raster with 16 bands, representing up to four cycles of potential disturbance and regrowth (disturbance year, corresponding disturbance anomaly percentile, regrowth year, corresponding regrowth anomaly percentile). 

From these inputs, a new band was derived to represent forest age. First, all non-forest areas were excluded (assigned a value of 0). For forest areas, the last year of regrowth was recorded. If disturbance was the most recent event, the pixel was reclassified as non-forest (value 0). If no disturbance was ever detected within the temporal coverage of the satellite imagery, the pixel was assigned a value of 1, representing undisturbed forest. For all other forest pixels, forest age was calculated as the number of years since the last regrowth event, by subtracting the year of last regrowth from 2023 (the upper extent of satellite imagery stack).

The final output was a single-layer raster in which:
\begin{itemize}
    \item 0 = non-forest or forest that was disturbed but never recovered,
    \item 1 = forest that has never been disturbed (within the satellite imagery limits),
    \item 2–29 = forest age (years since the last regrowth event).
\end{itemize}




\subsection{Variable calculation}

\subsubsection{Forest landscape variables}

All forest variables were calculated within a radius of 1000~m from the center of each research plot, corresponding to the upper limit of tropical plant species dispersal \citep{corlettSeedDispersalDistances2009}. Forest cover and forest connectivity were computed using the \texttt{landscapemetrics} R package \citep{hesselbarthLandscapemetricsOpensourceTool2019}.  

\textbf{Forest successional stage:}
The forest mosaic output was used to classify forests into two successional stages: early successional forest ($\leq$ 30 years) and late successional forest ($>$ 30 years). For each plot, both forest successional stages were expressed as the percentage of forest area within the 1000~m radius belonging to each successional stage. 

\textbf{Forest cover:}
All forest pixels were assigned a value of 1, while non- forest areas were assigned a value of 0. Forest cover was defined as the percentage of total area classified as forest within the 1000~m radius.

\textbf{Forest connectivity:}
Similarly to forest cover, for forest connectivity, the landscape was classified into two categories: forest and non-forest. Connectivity was defined as the mean euclidean nearest-neighbor distance between forest patches (eq. \ref{eq:eucledean}), where $ENN(\text{patch}_{ij})$ is the euclidean nearest-neighbor distance to each patch. This approach was chosen as it is commonly used in landscape ecology \citep{palmero2020changes, keeley2021connectivity}. A forest patch was defined as one or more contiguous forest pixels, adjacent according to the queen’s case. Forest connectivity was calculated using the \textit{lsm\_c\_enn\_mn()} function \citep{mcgarigal2002fragstats}. To ensure interpretability, the metric was inverted by taking its reciprocal ($1/ENN_{MN}$), so that higher values represent greater connectivity. 


\begin{align}
\label{eq:eucledean}
ENN_{MN} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} ENN(\text{patch}_{ij})
\end{align}

\textbf{Forest type:} Forest type was treated as a categorical variable with two classes: dry and wet, based on the characteristics of the research area.



\subsubsection{Seed variables}

\textbf{Seed richness:} Seed richness was defined as the count of unique plant seed species recorded per research plot.

\textbf{Seed dispersal mode:}
Seed dispersal mode refers to the mechanism by which seeds are dispersed \citep{dentUnitingNicheDifferentiation2021}. In this study, we distinguished between biotic and abiotic dispersal. Biotic dispersal is characterized by living dispersal agents such as animals. Abiotic dispersal is characterized by non-living dispersal agents, such as wind and water \citep{hordijkLandUseHistory2024}. This variable was represented by two proportions: the proportion of seeds dispersed biotically and the proportion dispersed abiotically. However, some seeds were characterized by both modes of dispersal. For clear distinction between the two modes, these seeds were excluded from the analysis. Additionally, seeds without a known dispersal mode were also excluded from the analysis.

\textbf{Seed guild:} Seed guild classifies plant species into functional groups based on their ecological strategies \citep{blondelGuildsFunctionalGroups2003}. We distinguished three guild types: pioneer, shade-tolerant, and generalist. Pioneer species are rapid colonizers that benefit from open and disturbed
landscapes \cite{poorterComprehensiveFrameworkVegetation2024}. Shade-tolerant species are specialists adapted to low light conditions
typically found in established forest communities \citep{dentUnitingNicheDifferentiation2021}. Generalist
species are widespread species able to exploit a wide range of resources, and often found in disturbed landscapes \citep{poorterComprehensiveFrameworkVegetation2024}. Seed guild was represented by three variables, each indicating the proportion of seeds belonging to the corresponding guild type.  \\







\subsection{Statistical analysis}
Spearman’s rank correlation was calculated separately for wet and dry forest types to assess pairwise associations among all variables \citep{bishara2012testing}. This method was chosen because some variables were non parametric, as tested with Shapiro test \citep{shapiro1972approximate}. Correlation matrices were visualized as heatmaps, with correlation coefficients and significance values reported using base R and the \texttt{ggplot2} R package.
 
To investigate how seed community properties are affected by forest landscape structural factors, several multiple linear regression models were performed. In this statistical analysis the following variables were considered as the predictor variables: forest cover, forest connectivity, forest early successional stage, forest late successional stage and forest type; and the following were considered as the response variables: seed richness, proportion of abiotic seed dispersal, proportion of biotic seed dispersal, proportion of generalist guild, proportion of shade-tolerant guild, and proportion of pioneer guild.
Prior the analysis, predictor variables representing forest cover, forest connectivity and forest early successional stage and forest late successional stage were scaled using the \textit{scale()} function from the \texttt{base} R package in order to improve comparability. Additionally, forest type was converted into a factor data type using the \textit{as.factor()} function from the \texttt{base} R package in order to be included in the linear regression model. 
Response variables representing biotic dispersal, abiotic dispersal and pioneer guild were transformed when necessary to meet model assumptions: square‐root transformation for biotic dispersal, squaring for abiotic dispersal and log(x+1) transformation for pioneer guild. Richness, biotic dispersal, abiotic dispersal, generalist, and pioneer guilds were modeled using linear regression models (LM). The shade-tolerant guild, however, was analyzed using zero-inflated beta (BEZI family) regression using the \texttt{gamlss} R package  to account for the high frequency of zeros. Model assumptions were evaluated through residual diagnostics (normality, homoscedasticity, and influential points). 

For each response variable (seed richness, biotic seed dispersal, abiotic seed dispersal, generalist, shade-tolerant, and pioneer species guilds), we tested three alternative models to evaluate the role of (i) overall forest cover, (ii) early successional forest, or (iii) late successional forest, in combination with forest connectivity and forest type. The separation of models was done due to the high multi-collinearity between forest cover and forest successional stage (VIF $\approx$ 10). For each model, the significance of predictors was evaluated using standard t-tests on regression coefficients, and overall model fit was assessed via the F-statistic and the proportion of explained variance (adjusted R²). 

Overview of the three types of the models:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Model 1}: $response\ variable \sim forest\  cover + forest\ connectivity + forest\ type$ 
    \item \textbf{Model 2}: $response\ variable \sim forest\ early\ successional\ stage + forest\ connectivity + forest\ type$
    \item \textbf{Model 3}: $response\ variable \sim forest\ late\ successional\ stage + forest\ connectivity + forest\ type$
\end{itemize}

To aid interpretation, model coefficients were summarized in regression table using the \textit{stargazer()} function from the \texttt{stargazer} R package \citep{hlavac2022package}, and predicted effects were visualized using the \texttt{ggeffects} package \citep{ludecke2020package}.
